Abstract. Zone is a binary-to-binary metamorphic engine for compact Windows
x86 and x64 payloads. Its instruction substitution, insertion, and control-flow
transformations include the Position-Independent Instruction representation
introduced in the conference version of this work. This extended article retains
the original design and empirical study of 102 inputs under fifteen configura-
tions, assessed through static-scan verdicts, functional checks, size, entropy, and
similarity. The published observations show that changes in detectability and
functional preservation must be evaluated separately. Building on the code–data
ambiguity identified in that study, we add a data-handling layer with symbolic
and heuristic identification and an alternative LLM-based classification path. We
also describe LLM-assisted assembly transformation with zero-shot and two-shot
conditions and integrate assembly wrapping as an existing technique, with its
x86 port under development. A separate assessment protocol addresses classi-
fication quality, construction success, working rate, static-scan measurements,
representation similarity, output diversity, and cost. The original results provide
the empirical foundation for the extension, while new campaigns are identified
independently. Conclusions remain bounded by the input scope, observation
protocol, and raw-binary scan setting.

2 Background
The following background distinguishes metamorphic rewriting from polymorphic en-
coding and introduces the transformation families used in the baseline evaluation.
2.1 Metamorphic Engines
A metamorphic engine constructs a different instruction-level representation of an input
program intended to preserve its behavior. Early virus generators used this idea to vary
their byte sequences between instances [25]. Functional preservation is a design objec-
tive and must be checked; it does not follow merely from the fact that a transformation
has produced an executable output.
A key difference is the absence of a mandatory decrypt-and-execute stage [29]. An
encryptedpayloadisnormallyrecoveredbeforeexecution,whileasuccessfullyrewritten
payloadcanexecuteinitstransformedrepresentation.Thischangeswhatamemoryscan
may observe, without implying that the transformed program is undetectable.
Instructionsubstitution,insertion,andreorderingcangeneratemanyrepresentations
from the same input [29,17]. This diversity complicates the use of fixed byte patterns,
and places a substantial burden on the rewriting engine to preserve control flow, data
references, and processor state. Transformation-based evasion and its limitations are
surveyed in [12].
Comparison with Polymorphism. Polymorphic encoding usually combines an encoded
body with a variable decoding routine [25]. Shikata Ga Nai is a representative example:
variation in its decoding stub changes the static byte sequence, but recurring features of
that stub can themselves be used for recognition [18]. Once decoding has completed,
the original body may be available to memory analysis. Metamorphic rewriting instead
changes the body that is executed [11]; this is a distinction between representations, not
a guarantee of runtime evasion.
2.2 Morphing Techniques
The established transformation families considered in Zone are EQ, NOP insertion,
IBP, and BCF. They can be evaluated separately or in selected combinations. Their an-
tecedents are discussed in Section13.
EQ changes the instruction vocabulary; NOP insertion adds operations intended to
leave the relevant computation unchanged. IBP modifies the physical layout of instruc-
tionblocks,whereasBCFintroducesbranchesandunreachableregions.PII,describedin
Section 3.1, further separates instruction placement from logical execution order. These
descriptions summarize the design reported in [4].

Instruction Block Permutation. IBP changes where instruction blocks are stored while
retaining their intended logical succession. The original implementation organizes the
transformation into the following stages:
1. Partitioning.The instruction sequence is divided into blocks; the chosen granular-
ity affects both rewriting overhead and the amount of layout variation.
2. Connecting blocks.An unconditional branch links each block to its logical succes-
sor.
3. Changing placement. Blocks are permuted, while the entry block retains its posi-
tion.
4. Updating references.Control-flow targets are adjusted to the resulting layout.
This transformation changes adjacency without necessarily changing most instruc-
tions within a block. Consequently, a detector based on a short pattern internal to an
unchanged region may behave differently from one that relies on a longer contiguous
sequence. The experiment assesses these changes empirically rather than assuming that
every signature is disrupted.

The implementation uses conditions with predetermined outcomes to exclude the in-

0x11–0x17.

3 Zone Architecture
The name Zone refers to Tarkovsky’sStalker and its alternative paths toward a common
destination.Theopen-sourceimplementation ¹wasoriginallyexploredforbothcomplete
PE files and raw payloads. The extended manuscript retains the payload-level scope of
the conference paper [4]; the difficulties of maintaining PE metadata are not evaluated
as a separate contribution.
The implementation is written in Python. Capstone disassembles the input, the se-
lected transformations modify the instruction representation, and Keystone assembles
the output. The bin2bin interface requires neither source code nor Program Database
(PDB) symbol files.
3.1 Position-Independent Instruction
PII was introduced in the conference paper [4]. It decouples the physical position of an
instruction from its place in the intended execution sequence.
Organization. Consider an instruction such asADD rax, 1 . In the PII representation
it is placed in a wrapper rather than relying on adjacency to its successor. The wrapper
executes the original operation and transfers control to a redirector. An offset table sup-
plies the location of the next logical instruction. Figure1 reproduces the overall layout,
while the listings below document the components of the original design.
The representation consists of four components:
1. Base-Address Stub (Stub1). This component determines the runtime base address
and records it in a designated memory location.

2. Redirector Stub (Stub2). This component obtains the next logical instruction lo-

3. Offset Table.Thiscontiguoustablecontainstheinstructionoffsetsusedbytheredi-
rector.
4. Wrapped Instructions. Each input instruction is placed in a wrapper intended to
maintain the required register, flag, and stack state.
Execution Sequence. The conference paper describes execution in three phases:
– Initialization. A call enters Stub1 (Listing1). The return address supplied by that
call is used to establish the payload’s runtime location.
– Original operation. The wrapper in Listing2 restores the saved state, adjusts the
stack, executes the input instruction, and supplies its logical index to Stub2.
– Dispatch. The redirector in Listing3 saves the state it uses, obtains the successor’s
location from the offset table and base address, and transfers control to that wrapper
after restoring the relevant state.
Physical Placement. The original implementation permutes complete wrappers, treat-
ing each five-instruction wrapper as a unit. The offset table is updated for the resulting
placement. Execution still starts with the initial call to Stub1; logical succession is rep-
resented by the table rather than by the wrappers’ physical adjacency.

3.2 How Instructions Are Patched
Moving instructions or inserting bytes can invalidate references to their previous loca-
tions.Thepatchingphaseupdatescontrol-flowdestinationsandotheraffectedreferences
tothemodifiedlayout.Branchesandcallsarethereforepartoftherewritingprocess,not
merely instructions that can be copied without regard to their new positions.
This requirement also explains the scope restriction. Rewriting raw instruction se-
quences already requires consistent target updates; complete PE files introduce addi-
tional metadata and layout dependencies. Algorithm1 reproduces the high-level work-
flow from [4].
3.3 Transformation Pipeline
The original workflow in Algorithm1 can be read as six stages. The additional analysis
and transformation paths are described in Sections8–10.
1. Input representation. Capstone produces instruction objects held in a linked se-
quence, with an address-indexed dictionary for lookup (Line4).
2. Embedded data. The implementation enables Capstone’s Skipdata option
(Line 5). This does not solve the general problem of identifying inline data,
particularly when data bytes also admit an instruction interpretation [10].
3. Reference tracking. A label table associates each tracked control-flow operation
with its target instruction object. This association is retained as the object’s physical
address changes.
4. Transformation and layout update. The chosen transformations are applied
(Line 19), followed by recomputation of instruction addresses (Line12).
5. Reassembly of tracked branches.Keystoneassemblesthetrackedcontrol-flowop-
erations using their updated targets (Line25).
6. Output. The resulting instruction bytes are written as a new raw binary (Line36).

6.1 Mitigations and Countermeasures
Changing the binary representation does not remove a program’s observable actions.
Defensive analysis can consider memory protections, execution patterns, API activity,
and network behavior in addition to static bytes. Recognition of a transformation engine
is another possibility: repeated artifacts of a common toolchain can persist across out-
puts, as documented for Shikata Ga Nai [18]. Statistical or learned detectors must also
account for benign programs that share similar features. These are defensive considera-
tions arising from the study, not results of an additional detection experiment.

8.2 Symbolic and Heuristic Identification
The non-LLM path combines static graph information with bounded symbolic explo-
ration. A CFGFast pass supplies candidate code blocks and inferred data objects. Sym-
bolic explorationrecords instruction addresses visited under the raw-payload model. An
additional graph-recovery pass can use newly observed entries, so that dynamically ob-
served code is not lost merely because it was absent from the first graph.
The symbolic initial state models uninitialized registers and memory symbolically.
It does not initialize a complete Windows process or implement all external API behav-
ior. Exploration is bounded by a time budget, a state/block-step limit, a pending-state
limit, and solver budgets. Reports record the stop reason and unresolved states. Reach-
ing a resource limit, encountering an unsupported operation, and exhausting the current
frontier have different meanings and are reported separately.
The implementation maintains evidence at byte granularity by expanding observed
instruction starts into their decoded spans. This avoids treating the interior bytes of an
observedinstructionasunexecuteddatacandidates.Candidatestringsarethenidentified
withprintableASCIIandUTF-16LEpatterns,includingtheirterminators.Overlapwith
observed code prevents acceptance under this string heuristic.
Static graph recovery can itself classify a string as code. For a string that overlaps
candidate graph code, the implementation requires additional structural corroboration
rather than relying on printability alone. Other proposed regions originate from graph-
inferreddataorfrombytesoutsiderecoveredblocks.Thelatterremainuncertainandare
distinguished from stronger data candidates.
The analysis returns intervals, their kinds, and diagnostic information; it does not
prove a complete partition of the payload into code and data. In particular, a byte not

8.3 LLM-Based Identification
The alternative identification path presents the input through a hex view, a linear disas-
sembly, and candidate printable runs. These are different views of the same bytes. The
disassembly is explicitly heuristic, and the printable runs are candidate evidence rather
than reference labels. The model returns structured data ranges, including offset and
length, instead of a rewritten binary.
The parser checks that intervals have positive lengths, remain within the input
bounds, and do not overlap. The engine then associates candidate ranges with its exist-
ing reference information. Ranges that cannot be accepted by the configured pipeline
are reported or rejected. A fresh engine instance is used when applying a proposal,
avoiding reuse of partially modified state after a rejected answer.
An invalid answer can trigger another request with feedback. The reported outcome
therefore distinguishes the first proposal, later proposals, and the final accepted result.
This distinction is necessary to attribute an observation correctly: a useful final extrac-
tion may reflect both the model’s proposal and deterministic filtering.
The number of worked examples in this classification prompt is recorded indepen-
dently of the transformation experiment. A zero-shot/two-shot comparison of assembly
transformation does not imply that the same comparison has been performed for identi-
fication.

Different architectures require different addressing treatment. The x64 and x86 im-

been recovered. An unsupported case is consequently distinguished from a successfully
handled case and from an output that is built but fails execution.
Analysis caching is a practical part of the implementation. Candidate data can be
reused for an input identity, while references are recomputed against the working repre-

8.5 Classification Outputs and Residual Uncertainty
Three outputs are retained for analysis: raw candidate intervals, intervals accepted by
deterministic checks, and intervals ultimately processed by the data layer. Equal final
binaries do not establish equal raw classifications, because filtering can remove differ-
ences. Conversely, a correct candidate can be rejected because its reference pattern is
unsupported. That rejection is an integration limitation, not necessarily a classification
error.
The additional evaluation in Section11 therefore separates classification quality
from downstream functionality. Successful execution supplies evidence about the ob-
served behavior after data handling, while reference annotations supply evidence about
the byte roles. Neither observation can substitute for the other.
9 LLM-Assisted Assembly Transformation
9.1 Task and Supported Transformation Families
The transformation path asks an LLM to produce a complete modified assembly repre-
sentation for a selected family: EQ, NOP insertion, BCF, or IBP. These families retain
their meanings from the native engine; the use of a model changes how the proposed
representation is produced. Each request selects one family so that the generated artifact
and its validation policy have an identifiable experimental condition.
PII and assembly wrapping remain native paths in the version examined here. They
arenotdescribedasLLM-generatedtechniques.Likewise,anativeconditioncombining
transformations is not automatically equivalent to several model calls in sequence. The
experiment identifies the implemented path rather than grouping all uses of the same
family under one label.
9.2 Assembly Representation and Data Boundary
The engine prepares an assembly representation with symbolic labels for the relevant
locations and preserves the data representation outside the model-generated instruction
text. This preparation defines an intermediate input to the transformation task. It also
determinesthereferencepointforseveralstructuralchecksandsimilaritymeasurements.
The model’s text is assembled and associated with the data retained by the engine.
The output is therefore a product of model generation, assembly, data handling, and
validation. The experimental record retains the identity of the original file, the inter-
mediate representation, and the final bytes, so that preparation-induced changes can be
distinguished from changes in the model’s output.
9.3 Zero-Shot and T wo-Shot Conditions
The initial zero-shot request contains the task specification without worked examples.
The initial two-shot request contains two examples in addition to that specification. The
example identities and prompt versions are part of the experimental condition. Differ-
ences in their content, not only their number, can influence the comparison.

9.4 Validation and Feedback
Validation covers several kinds of structural evidence. Assembly must succeed, the pro-
duced code must meet the decoding checks, tracked branch destinations must have ap-
propriate boundaries, and the expected data representation must remain consistent. Ad-
ditional checks depend on the selected family and examine whether the output still con-
tains the required instruction structure or applies a nontrivial transformation.
These checks are deliberately narrower than semantic equivalence. An output can
pass structural validation yet behave differently; a conservative check can also reject an
output that would have performed the expected behavior. The assessment records the
reason for each rejection rather than treating all rejected answers as one category.
For supported test cases, a functional verifier can participate in the generation loop.

9.5 What Is Attributed to the Model
Three aspects are considered separately: whether an answer can be assembled, whether
the complete pipeline accepts it, and whether an accepted output satisfies the reported
functional check. Deterministic preparation and validation are part of the system contri-
bution rather than capabilities attributed to the model alone.
The run record includes elapsed time, API usage when supplied by the service, re-
jected attempts, and the accepted artifact. Estimated cost is derived from the stated tariff
snapshot; it is not presented as an invoice. Resumed jobs and reused outputs are identi-
fied so that they are not counted as additional generation calls.

The relevant conceptual property is that the instruction interpretation obtained by a
particular static decoding need not be the instruction stream followed during execution.
This property makes it especially important to identify which representation is used by
a similarity or coverage measurement. A statistic from the static view and one from the
intended execution view answer different questions.
10.2 Integration and Supported Inputs
Wrapping is integrated with the native rewriting infrastructure and depends on an in-
struction representation adequate for the selected input. It must also interact with the
final data layout and the references affected by changes in instruction placement. Con-
struction can be refused when the required input interpretation is incomplete or an un-
supported case is encountered.
Some instructions or local regions are handled differently from the main wrapped
representation. The assessment therefore records the proportion represented by wrap-
ping, retained regions, output size, construction failures, and functional outcomes. A
claim about the whole output must not be inferred solely from the fraction of instruc-
tions accepted by one internal stage.
The experiment distinguishes wrapping alone, the chosen combined native condi-
tion, and the corresponding preparation-only controls. Their names identify complete
configurations, including data handling and architecture. Implementation availability
and successful build are reported separately from empirical functionality.
10.3 x86 Port and Development Status
The x64 implementation is available for assessment. The x86 port is under active devel-
opment at the time of this draft. Register width, instruction encodings, and addressing
assumptionsrequirearchitecture-specifictreatment,soanx64outcomeisnottransferred
to the x86 column of a result table.
The result layout reserves separate x86 entries. Those entries remain marked as
development work until a frozen version and its assessment are available. If the port
changes during a campaign, its new results are associated with the new version; they are
not silently appended to results from an earlier implementation.
10.4 Assessment Boundary
Wrapping is assessed with the same separation of construction, functionality, and rep-
resentation measurements used for other methods. In particular, a failure to construct an
output remains part of the overall outcome. Reporting functionality only for constructed
binaries can describe accepted artifacts but does not describe availability across all at-
tempted cases. Section11 defines both denominators.

contributions are the starting point of this manuscript, rather than new claims of the
extended version.
The extensions additionally connect data identification with bounded symbolic
and static analysis [2], and distinguish LLM classification from assembly transforma-
tion. Assembly wrapping is attributed to its existing description and acknowledged
antecedents in overlapping instructions [1]; its integration into Zone is assessed as an
implementation contribution. These new directions build on the earlier payload-level
study while preserving its detailed results for comparison.
14 Conclusions
Zone provides a binary-to-binary setting for studying metamorphic transformation of
compact Windows payloads. The original study of 102 inputs and fifteen configurations
remains the empirical foundation of this extended article. Its detection, functionality,
size, entropy, and similarity results expose different effects of rewriting: lower raw-file

representationoverhead.ThePIIdesignandtheSGNcomparisonareretainedwithinthe
scope of those published experiments.
The extension develops the code–data limitation identified by that study into an
explicit identification and data-handling layer. Symbolic and heuristic analysis and an
LLM-based alternative feed deterministic handling of accepted candidates. A separate
LLM path supports assembly transformation with zero-shot and two-shot conditions,
validation, and feedback. Integration of assembly wrapping adds an existing technique
to the engine; its x86 port remains under development pending architecture-specific as-
sessment.
