Abstract. Rewriting a raw binary requires distinguishing instructions from em-
bedded data and preserving their references when the layout changes. Zone is a
binary-to-binary metamorphic engine for compact Windows x86 and x64 pay-
loads. This extended study adds symbolic/heuristic and LLM-based data identi-
fication, LLM-proposed instruction transformations, and integration of assembly
wrapping, an existing technique that gives stored bytes different static and ex-
ecuted instruction interpretations. Six annotated benign programs are identified
exactly in 18 LLM and six symbolic runs. On 13 separate benign fixtures, LLM
transformation yields correct output buffers in 167/168 x64 and 138/144 x86 in-
vocations. On a separate recovery cohort of 13 previously problematic x64 pay-
loads, working rate improves from 1.2% to 80.2%; the corresponding x86 subset
improves from 14.9% to 25.9%. In the completed campaigns on all 102 original
payloads, wrapping constructs 243/306 variants, with 170 passing the functional
harness; LLM transformation constructs 350 outputs across 816 conditions, with
275 passing. Current raw-file scans give a mean engine detection rate of 0% for
207 distinct wrapping outputs and 5.06% across 322 nonempty reports for LLM-
transformed outputs. Together, the experiments demonstrate improved functional
reliability and evaluate the added transformation modes through construction, ex-
ecution, and static detection measurements.

1 Introduction
Metamorphic rewriting changes a program’s instruction sequence, whereas polymor-
phic encoding typically changes an encrypted body and its decoding routine. Both
complicate byte-pattern recognition, but expose different representations during execu-
tion [12,5,20]. Zone is a modularbin2bin engine for studying these effects on Windows
x86 and x64 payloads without source code or debugging symbols.

Motivation and Contributions of the Extension. The conference study [4] evaluated 102
payloads under 15 transformation configurations and observed substantial functional
loss. Embedded data is one source of failure: a disassembler can interpret a string as in-

when changes invalidate their references. This extension addresses data preparation and
evaluates additional ways to transform the prepared code. Its contributions are:
– Alternative symbolic/heuristic and LLM data classifiers, followed by shared deter-
ministic handling of supported data and references.
– AnLLMtransformationpathforEQ,NOP,BCF,andIBP,comparingrequestswith-

The original architecture, PII design, experiments, and comparison with Shikata Ga
Nai provide the baseline. Reused figures, tables, listings, and the algorithm carry source
citations; added methods and measurements are identified separately. The selected x64
recovery cohort improves from 1.2% to 80.2% working rate, motivating a joint assess-
ment of construction and execution rather than detection alone.
The scope remains compact, position-independent payloads. Full Portable Exe-

alignment, and entry points, and signatures may also occur outside code [21]. Raw-file

2 Background
The following background distinguishes metamorphic rewriting from polymorphic en-
coding and introduces the transformation families used in the baseline evaluation.
2.1 Metamorphic Engines
A metamorphic engine rewrites an instruction-level representation with functional
preservation as its objective. Substitution, insertion, and reordering produce different
byte sequences but require preservation of control flow, references, and relevant pro-
cessor state [24,28,17]. Unlike polymorphic encoding, metamorphism does not require
a decrypt-and-execute stage. Shikata Ga Nai varies its decoding stub, whose recurring
features can themselves be recognized [18]; after decoding, its original body may be
available to memory analysis [11]. A rewritten body instead executes in its transformed
representation, without this distinction alone guaranteeing runtime evasion.

2.2 Morphing Techniques
The established transformation families considered in Zone are EQ, NOP insertion,
IBP, and BCF. They can be evaluated separately or in selected combinations. Their an-
tecedents are discussed in Section14.
EQ changes the instruction vocabulary; NOP insertion adds operations intended to
leave the relevant computation unchanged. IBP modifies the physical layout of instruc-
tionblocks,whereasBCFintroducesbranchesandunreachableregions.PII,describedin
Section 3.1, further separates instruction placement from logical execution order. These
descriptions summarize the design reported in [4].
Equal Code Substitution. EQ replaces an operation with an alternative representation
intended to have the same effect. For example, the zeroing operationsXOR rax, rax
and MOV rax, 0x0 have different encodings. Such examples illustrate changes in byte
representation; preservation of all relevant state remains an implementation concern.
Table 1 collects the substitution examples used in the conference study, including
arithmetic complements and stack-based sequences. Instruction substitution and its use
with insertion transformations have a long history [7,8,22].
NOP/Dead Code Insertion. Insertion transformations introduce operations intended to
leave the program’s relevant behavior unchanged. The single-byteNOP is one example;
Table2 reproduces the other examples considered in the original implementation.
Some apparent no-ops, including arithmetic with zero, can change processor flags.
The original implementation therefore used flag-preserving sequences around such op-
erations,asillustratedinTable 3.Theresultingrepeatedpatternsarethemselvesrelevant
to detection: changes introduced by an engine can create recognizable regularities [3].
Instruction Block Permutation. IBP changes where instruction blocks are stored while
retaining their intended logical succession. The original implementation organizes the
transformation into the following stages:
1. Partitioning.The instruction sequence is divided into blocks; the chosen granular-
ity affects both rewriting overhead and the amount of layout variation.
2. Connectingblocks. An unconditional branch links each block to its logical succes-
sor.
3. Changing placement.Blocks are permuted, while the entry block retains its posi-
tion.

3.1 Position-Independent Instruction
PII was introduced in the conference paper [4]. It decouples the physical position of an
instruction from its place in the intended execution sequence.
Organization. Consider an instruction such asADD rax, 1 . In the PII representation
it is placed in a wrapper rather than relying on adjacency to its successor. The wrapper
executes the original operation and transfers control to a redirector. An offset table sup-
plies the location of the next logical instruction. Figure1 reproduces the overall layout,
while the listings below document the components of the original design.
The representation consists of four components:

6 S. Bistarelli et al.
1. Base-AddressStub(Stub1). This component determines the runtime base address
and records it in a designated memory location.
2. Redirector Stub (Stub2).This component obtains the next logical instruction lo-
cation from the offset table and dispatches execution to it.
3. OffsetTable. Thiscontiguoustablecontainstheinstructionoffsetsusedbytheredi-
rector.
4. Wrapped Instructions.Each input instruction is placed in a wrapper intended to
maintain the required register, flag, and stack state.
Execution Sequence. The conference paper describes execution in three phases:
– Initialization. A call enters Stub1 (Listing1). The return address supplied by that
call is used to establish the payload’s runtime location.
– Original operation.The wrapper in Listing2 restores the saved state, adjusts the
stack, executes the input instruction, and supplies its logical index to Stub2.
– Dispatch. The redirector in Listing3 saves the state it uses, obtains the successor’s
location from the offset table and base address, and transfers control to that wrapper
after restoring the relevant state.
Physical Placement. The original implementation permutes complete wrappers, treat-
ing each five-instruction wrapper as a unit. The offset table is updated for the resulting
placement. Execution still starts with the initial call to Stub1; logical succession is rep-
resented by the table rather than by the wrappers’ physical adjacency.

3.2 How Instructions Are Patched
Moving instructions or inserting bytes can invalidate references to their previous loca-
tions.Thepatchingphaseupdatescontrol-flowdestinationsandotheraffectedreferences
tothemodifiedlayout.Branchesandcallsarethereforepartoftherewritingprocess,not
merely instructions that can be copied without regard to their new positions.
This requirement also explains the scope restriction. Rewriting raw instruction se-
quences already requires consistent target updates; complete PE files introduce addi-
tional metadata and layout dependencies. Algorithm1 reproduces the high-level work-
flow from [4].
3.3 Transformation Pipeline
The original workflow in Algorithm1 can be read as six stages. The additional analysis
and transformation paths are described in Sections8–10.
1. Input representation.Capstone produces instruction objects held in a linked se-
quence, with an address-indexed dictionary for lookup (Line4).
2. Embedded data. The implementation enables Capstone’s Skipdata option
(Line 5). This does not solve the general problem of identifying inline data,
particularly when data bytes also admit an instruction interpretation [10].
3. Reference tracking.A label table associates each tracked control-flow operation
with its target instruction object. This association is retained as the object’s physical
address changes.
4. Transformation and layout update. The chosen transformations are applied
(Line 19), followed by recomputation of instruction addresses (Line12).
5. Reassemblyoftrackedbranches. Keystoneassemblesthetrackedcontrol-flowop-
erations using their updated targets (Line25).
6. Output. The resulting instruction bytes are written as a new raw binary (Line36).

6.1 Mitigations and Countermeasures
Changing the binary representation does not remove a program’s observable actions.
Defensive analysis can consider memory protections, execution patterns, API activity,
and network behavior in addition to static bytes. Recognition of a transformation engine
is another possibility: repeated artifacts of a common toolchain can persist across out-
puts, as documented for Shikata Ga Nai [18]. Statistical or learned detectors must also
account for benign programs that share similar features. These are defensive considera-
tions arising from the study, not results of an additional detection experiment.

8.3 Symbolic Exploration Combined with Static Heuristics
The non-LLM method combines three sources of evidence. First, static analysis recov-
ers a control-flow graph (CFG): nodes represent basic blocks of instructions and edges
represent possible transfers of execution. Zone uses angr’s CFGFast analysis for this
initial structure [2]. Second, symbolic execution explores possible paths while repre-
senting unknown register and memory values as symbolic expressions. This supplies
additional evidence about which instructions can be reached. Third, pattern-based rules
recognize printable ASCII and UTF-16LE string candidates, including their termina-
tors, and consider regions identified as data by the control-flow analysis. Candidates
overlapping confirmed instruction bytes are filtered out. These rules supply recogniz-
able patterns and supporting evidence; an unexplored region alone is not sufficient to
classify it as data.

8.4 LLM Identification and Shared Data Handling
The LLM receives hexadecimal bytes, a linear disassembly, and candidate printable
strings, and returns structured data intervals. The disassembly is evidence to assess, not
a trusted annotation. This mode neither extracts data nor rewrites instructions.
Deterministic parsing rejects empty or out-of-bounds intervals and invalid overlaps.
The engine examines supported references and retains accepted data separately from
the instructions. After transformation, it finalizes references using the new layout. In-
tegrity checks compare retained bytes with their saved originals at the final locations
and verify supported references against their expected destinations; mismatches reject
the construction. Address handling is architecture-specific.

10 Assembly Wrapping: Mechanism and Integration
10.1 Reading the Same Bytes from Different Starting Points
Assembly wrapping relies on a property of x86 and x64: instructions have variable
lengths, and the starting byte determines how a decoder groups the following bytes into
instructions. The same stored bytes can therefore have two interpretations when read
from different starting points.
Consider two views of one region. In theouter view, a decoder sees an instruction
followed by its immediate operand, a constant stored inside that instruction. In theex-
ecution view, part of that constant’s byte field is read as an instruction sequence. The
established wrapping technique uses this overlap to give the stored representation an
outer instruction structure while retaining an intended executable interpretation within
it [1].
Figure 8 shows these two readings of one byte field. The termcontainer refers to
the outer instruction;embedded sequence refers to the instructions represented inside
its operand bytes. These are two readings of shared bytes, rather than two copies of the
program. No decryption step is required to obtain the embedded interpretation.
10.2 What Zone Adds and What the Transformation Requires
The contribution of this work is the integration and assessment of an existing technique,
with implementations for both architectures. Zone starts from a prepared instruction
stream, groups supported instructions into wrapped regions, preserves the required exe-
cution relationships, and updates the supported location-dependent references after lay-
out. Some instructions or local layouts cannot be placed in the main container represen-
tation.Forexample,aninstructionsequencelongerthantheavailableoperandfielddoes
not fit inside that container. The implementation retains unsupported cases in ordinary,
unwrapped regions. An output may therefore contain both wrapped and ordinary code.
Thex86andx64implementationssharethispurposebutusedifferentencodingsand
addressing rules. In particular, the available immediate operand has a different width,
affecting which instruction groups fit. Both architectures are included in the functional
evaluation.

The controlled suites help explain what each stage contributes. All six annotated
programs are classified exactly, while the 13 functional programs check the output of
individual programming patterns. Their small size and simple layouts make them useful
diagnostic tests. Their high success rates are not a consequence of being benign: the
original corpus contains larger and more varied instruction/data layouts, which exercise
additional cases. The recorded native x86 IBP result remains associated with its tested
version; a future corrected implementation requires a separately identified evaluation.
The two LLM roles remain distinct. Data identification feeds shared handling, giv-
ing identical binaries in 16/19 original cases and matching 17/19 functional outcomes.
Assemblytransformationproposesanewlisting;zero-shotandtwo-shothavesimilarag-
gregate outcomes. Input size, construction success, and execution outcome offer more
informative distinctions in this campaign.
The empirical scope can be stated once. Functional preservation is assessed through
the specified execution tests, rather than a formal proof for every possible execution.
Classification accuracy is measured on independently annotated programs; the unan-
notated payload comparison measures agreement and downstream functionality. Static
detection rates describe the submitted raw files and the reported engine responses, in-
cluding the disclosed unsupported and failed analyses. Repeated inputs, shared hashes,
recorded version differences, and retries are retained in the artifact so that these descrip-
tive results can be inspected and reproduced.
14 Related Work
Instruction-level obfuscation has been studied in both offensive and defensive set-
tings[25,6].Earlierengines,includingW95/Regswap[ 8]andMetaPHOR[ 23],explored
register substitution and code reorganization. Instruction substitution, insertion, and
control-flow transformations underpin the techniques summarized here [7,8,22,10,15].
Polymorphic encoding provides a related but distinct representation strategy. Anal-
yses of SGN describe both the variation in its decoding stub and opportunities to recog-
nize it [9,18]; memory-extraction research addresses the body recovered during execu-
tion[11].Formetamorphicprograms,detectionstudieshaveconsideredHiddenMarkov
Models [26,8], API-trace similarity [28], and genetic-algorithm-based methods [14].
More recent work examines LLM-based assembly transformation, including the
MetamorphASM benchmark and its MAD dataset [19,16]. Compiler-based systems
such as Obfuscator-LLVM [15] offer another setting, with access to source-level or
intermediate information during compilation.
The contribution of the conference paper was its payload-levelbin2bin study, the
PII design, and the joint evaluation of selected transformation configurations [4]. Those
contributions are the starting point of this manuscript, rather than new claims of the
extended version.
The extensions additionally connect data identification with bounded symbolic
and static analysis [2], and distinguish LLM classification from assembly transforma-
tion. Assembly wrapping is attributed to its existing description and acknowledged
antecedents in overlapping instructions [1]; its integration into Zone is assessed as an

study while preserving its detailed results for comparison.
15 Conclusions
The extended Zone pipeline makes data identification and supported reference handling
explicit before instruction transformation. It offers two distinct LLM roles—proposing
data regions and proposing rewritten assembly—and integrates assembly wrapping for
both x64 and x86. The original PII design and conference experiments remain the foun-
dation of this broader assessment.
The recovery experiment demonstrates a substantial gain on the selected previously
low-rate payloads: working rate increases from 1.2% to 80.2% on x64 and from 14.9%
to 25.9% on x86. The controlled tests give exact data identification on six annotated
programs and correct outputs in 167/168 x64 and 138/144 x86 LLM transformation
requests.
Acrossall102originalpayloads,wrappingproduces243binariesand170successful
functional tests from 306 planned variants. LLM transformation produces 350 binaries
and 275 successful tests across 816 configurations. Two-shot prompting shows no con-
sistent advantage over zero-shot in these observations. Static scans flag none of the 207
distinct wrapping outputs; LLM-transformed outputs have a mean engine detection rate
of 5.06% across 322 nonempty reports. Together, these results quantify the recovered
functionality and the behavior of the new transformation paths while identifying the
remaining architecture-dependent cases for future study.
